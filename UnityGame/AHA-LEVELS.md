# Little discoveries: six authored puzzles

Levels 16–21, accessible through **Map → New puzzles**. Earlier levels and saved scores are preserved. These are handcrafted layouts, not random generation.

## Design intent

Each puzzle has a concrete spatial or ordering discovery. All six fill their bags exactly, so spare volume cannot hide a poor arrangement. Rotation is demanded by geometry where relevant, rather than awarding a star merely for pressing Turn or Tip. Three stars also require no crushed groceries; the last two require one face-connected chilled group in a single bag.

Cold means the existing milk, yogurt and frozen peas. Eggs and ordinary produce are not silently made cold. Corner contact does not connect products; contact through a non-cold product does not connect them either. This is a packing convention, not a temperature simulation.

The solutions below are authored constructions reasoned against the source rules, **not executed solver results or playtest findings**. Alternative solutions are welcome. We have not established uniqueness or human difficulty.

## 16. The narrow strip

**Bag:** 3 wide × 2 deep × 1 high. **Order:** three loaves. **Volume:** 6/6.

**Discovery:** two broad loaves leave a one-cell-wide strip; the third fits lengthways.

With unchanged 2-wide footprints, at most one loaf fits in each of the two rows. Height one excludes standing a loaf on end. At least one horizontal turn is necessary, regardless of placement order.

**Construction:** place ordinary loaves at x=0 in front and back, then a turned 1-wide × 2-deep loaf at x=2. All sit on the floor. No load is carried by another grocery.

**Watch during playtesting:** Is the remaining strip readable? Does the player discover Turn without being told the answer?

## 17. The breakfast lid

**Bag:** 3 × 2 × 2. **Order:** cornflakes, milk, peas, yogurt, rice. **Volume:** 12/12.

**Discovery:** the large carton becomes a lid; the smaller groceries form its supported base. Choose an item behind the first conveyor item.

The cornflakes cannot remain three cells tall. A tempting full-floor carton base must carry six weight units, exceeding its capacity of three. A damage-free solution can instead place it last.

**Construction:** tip milk to 2 × 1 × 1 at x=0/front; place peas at x=0/back; yogurt at x=2/front and rice at x=2/back. Tip/turn cornflakes to 3 × 1 × 2 and place at y=1. Its weight of one is distributed across all six support cells; even yogurt is safe.

**Conveyor choices:** milk, peas, yogurt, rice, cornflakes. All are selectable within the first three remaining items at each step.

**Watch:** Does the cereal-as-lid discovery feel different from simply laying it flat? If the first attempt crushes the base, is the cause understandable?

## 18. Soft landing

**Bag:** 2 × 2 × 2. **Order:** yogurt, can, can, yogurt, can, can, yogurt, yogurt. **Volume:** 8/8.

**Discovery:** an item arriving first does not have to be packed first. Build the entire heavy base, then place the soft products.

All pieces are single cells and the filled bag has four columns of height two. A can weighs three, greater than yogurt's capacity of two. A can may never rest on yogurt in a three-star solution. The intended construction puts a can beneath every yogurt, but safe can-can and yogurt-yogurt columns are also valid. This lesson teaches weight order without requiring a unique arrangement.

**Construction and conveyor choices:** select four cans onto the four floor cells, then all four yogurts above them. Each can is available among the first three remaining items. Cans have unlimited capacity.

**Watch:** Does the player use selection order rather than assuming a fixed conveyor sequence? This is a short weight lesson, not a difficult geometry puzzle.

## 19. Save the headroom

**Bag:** 2 × 2 × 4. **Order:** flowers, cereal, cereal, flowers, flowers, cereal, rice. **Volume:** 16/16.

**Discovery:** lay two small cereal boxes flat to create a low platform for upright bouquets.

Three bouquets occupy three columns for three levels each and carry no load. Without tipping any cereal, the three flower columns cannot hold a two-high carton beneath them; the remaining column cannot hold all six cereal cells plus rice. Thus a fully upright arrangement cannot work.

**Construction:** two tipped cereal boxes make a complete floor, one 2-wide box per row. Put bouquets on three of the four cells at y=1, reaching the top at y=4. In the fourth column, place the last cereal upright at y=1 and rice at y=3. The rear foundation cereal carries one flower plus the last cereal and rice: four units, below its capacity of five. The upper cereal carries only the two-unit rice.

**Conveyor choices:** cereal, cereal, flowers, flowers, flowers, cereal, rice. All choices fit the three-item window.

**Watch:** Is the one-level platform discovery legible? The level may be harder than the previous three because it combines headroom with support.

## 20. A chilled corner

**Bag:** 3 × 2 × 2. **Order:** milk, cereal, rice, peas, rice, yogurt, rice, rice, rice. **Volume:** 12/12.

**Discovery:** reserve a compact connected section for chilled groceries; use the pantry items to fill around it.

**Construction:** front row: upright milk at x=0; peas on the floor at x=1–2; yogurt above peas at x=1 and rice above peas at x=2. Back row: a tipped cereal box at x=0–1 on the floor, rice at x=2 on the floor, then rice in all three upper back cells. Milk touches peas, and yogurt touches peas and milk. Peas carry three units (capacity four); cereal carries four (capacity five); the rear rice stack carries two (capacity eight).

**Conveyor choices:** milk, cereal, rice (back x=2 floor), peas, rice (front x=2 upper), yogurt, remaining three rice across upper back row.

**Why cold matters:** a geometrically valid alternative uses two rice cubes as a vertical divider at x=1/front, milk at x=0/front, upright peas at x=2/front, yogurt on the back floor at x=0, rice at back-floor x=1 and x=2, a flat cereal over the first two back cells, and rice over the last. Milk/yogurt and peas are then separate cold groups despite filling the bag safely. Cold connectivity earns the third star; space alone is insufficient.

**Watch:** Does the player recognize face contact? This is a grouping lesson; it does not force yogurt to be the unique connector.

## 21. Two different jobs

**Bags:** two bags, each 2 × 2 × 3. **Order:** flowers, rice, cornflakes, milk, rice, flowers, peas, rice, flowers, yogurt, rice. **Volume:** 24/24.

**Discovery:** separate jobs by bag: upright flowers and pantry rice in one; the family box and a chilled section in the other.

**Bag A:** three bouquets occupy three floor columns, each to the top. Stack three rice boxes in the fourth column. The bottom rice carries four, the middle two; both are safe.

**Bag B:** turn the family box to 1 wide × 2 deep × 3 high at x=0. At x=1, place upright milk in front and rice behind on the floor. Yogurt goes above that rice. Turn peas to 1 wide × 2 deep × 1 high and place them across milk and yogurt at y=2. Peas distribute their weight equally: milk carries one, yogurt carries one, rice carries two. The three chilled items all connect, with no chilled groceries in Bag A.

**Conveyor sequence with destinations:** flower A, rice A, cornflakes B, milk B, rice A, flower A, rice A, flower A, rice B, yogurt B, peas B. Every choice is within the first-three window. Switching bags happens before selecting a grocery; no cross-bag transfer while holding one is required.

**Watch:** Does specialization emerge naturally? Can players recover with Undo and bag switching? This is the longest combination puzzle; do not treat its difficulty as calibrated yet.

## Algorithm decision

No generator is needed for this six-level pass. The existing optional solver is useful later to check solvability and rule violations, but was not run because testing is reserved for the user. A future generator should produce candidates around a selected lesson, retain a valid packing and conveyor sequence, and reject trivial alternatives. Human playtests must still determine whether the discovery is satisfying.

For feedback, note the first arrangement tried, the moment of discovery, whether a hint was needed, and whether failure felt understandable. A second valid solution is not automatically a design flaw.
